# Claude Hopkins' principles

Distilled from a Founders podcast episode on Claude Hopkins' *Scientific Advertising* and *My Life in Advertising*, using the full transcript. Each principle has the source idea, the quote, and what it means for a SocialSpark ad. The episode's biographical material (work ethic, work as play, his rise under Albert Lasker) is left out because it doesn't affect how an ad is written, except where it sets up a principle (#12, #20).

## 1. Advertising is salesmanship in print — treat people as you would in person

> "We must treat people in advertising as we treat them in person. Center on their desires."
> "My everlasting argument was against dealing with people in the mass… However big your business, get down to the units."

Hopkins' sewing-machine example: instead of mailing every inquirer the general catalogue, send the sewing-machine catalogue, plus the names of neighbours who own one so she can see it and ask them.

**Apply:** write to one agent with one listing. Match the ad to the search or the audience segment exactly (an "open house flyer" search gets an open-house ad, a broker gets a broker ad). Where proof exists, make it local and checkable.

## 2. Service, never selfishness

> "The average salesman seeks favors… His plea is, 'Buy my goods.' He makes a selfish appeal to selfish people, and of course, he meets resistance. I was selling service."
> "Argue anything for your own advantage and people will resist to the limit. But seem unselfishly to consider your customers' desires and they will naturally flock to you."
> "You cannot go into a well-occupied field on the simple appeal of 'buy my brand.'"

**Apply:** the reader is the subject of every sentence. "Your listing, posted tonight" not "SocialSpark generates posts". To win agents away from habits (Canva, a VA, doing nothing) the ad must offer clearly *more* service, not louder claims.

## 3. The two great faults: boasts and selfishness

> "The greatest two faults in advertising lie in boasts and in selfishness. People will listen if you talk service to them. They will turn their backs when you seek to impress an advantage for yourself."
> The maker "tells of things that he takes pride in. The size of his plant or the age of his business… The advertising man must study the consumer and tell the consumer what he wants to know."

**Apply:** cut "leading", "#1", "AI-powered platform", "built by…". Keep what the agent wants to know: what they get, how fast, what it costs, what could go wrong and why it won't.

## 4. Tell the pains you take (the Schlitz story)

Schlitz was fifth in market share. Hopkins toured the brewery: plate-glass rooms of filtered air, wood-pulp filters, pumps cleaned twice daily, bottles washed four times, 4,000-ft artesian wells, 1,200 experiments to develop the yeast. Schlitz said every brewer did this. Hopkins: "Others have never told this story… It will startle everyone in print. I gave purity a meaning." Schlitz rose to neck-and-neck with first place within months.

> "The maker is too close to his product. He sees in his methods only the ordinary. He does not realize that the world at large might marvel at those methods."

**Apply:** our "ordinary" engineering is the story. Photo selection from the listing, voice learned from past posts, Québec French as a real language option, Canadian semi-annual compounding, CMHC premiums including the 30-year surcharge, minimum down payments enforced automatically, the AI never writes the numbers, licence number and "as of" date on every sheet, data stored in Canada, nothing posted without approval. Give "accurate" and "safe" a meaning.

## 5. A good product is its own best salesman — samples and demonstration

Selling his mother's silver polish door to door, Hopkins sold about one woman in ten by talking, nearly all when he could demonstrate.

> "It is uphill work to sell goods without samples."
> "No argument in the world can ever compare with one dramatic demonstration."

But:

> "I have never found that it paid to give samples to people who do not request it… Products handed out without asking lose respect. It is different when you force people to make an effort."

**Apply:** the offer is a demonstration: paste one listing link, see your branded post free. The small effort (pasting a link, creating an account) is a feature. In creative, show before → after: the listing link and the finished carousel or feature sheet.

## 6. Offer to take the chance yourself

> "Ask a person to take a chance on you and you have a fight. Offer to take a chance on him and the way is easy."
> "Serve better than others. Offer more help than others and you are pretty sure to win."

**Apply:** free posts, no credit card, cancel anytime, edit everything, nothing posts until you click. Put the risk reversal in the ad, not only on the landing page.

## 7. Tell the truth, in a fascinating way (John Powers)

> "He told the truth, but told it in a rugged and fascinating way."

Powers' bankrupt-clothier ad: "We are bankrupt. We owe $125,000 more than we can pay… If you come and buy tomorrow, we shall have the money to meet them." Truth was so rare it caused a sensation.

**Apply:** candour sells. Admitting a limit builds belief in everything else (e.g. "REALTOR.ca blocks tools like ours — so copy and paste the page and it works anyway"). Plain words, no hype.

## 8. Never judge humanity by yourself — submit everything to the public

> "We must never judge humanity by ourselves. The things we want, the things we like may appeal to a small minority."
> "We must submit all things in advertising to the court of public opinion… I own an ocean-going yacht, but do you suppose I venture across the ocean without a chart or a compass?"

**Apply:** deliver distinct appeals and a test plan, not one "best" ad. Decide winners by cost per sign-up or per first generated post, never by taste.

## 9. Stay close to the buyer; learn from salesmen

> "Study salesmen, canvassers, and fakers if you want to know how to sell goods."
> Some of the best copywriters Hopkins knew could barely write grammatically, "but he knew people, and the people bought what he had to sell."

**Apply:** use agents' own words ("Just Listed", "open house this weekend", "price improvement", "my brokerage", "my headshot"), not product words ("generation", "template engine"). If the user has customer quotes, support tickets or reviews, mine them for phrasing.

## 10. Headlines pick out the reader and carry the main appeal

> "The purpose of a headline is to pick out people you can interest. You wish to talk to someone in a crowd. So the first thing you say is, 'Hey there, Bill Jones.'"
> "People are hurried… There may be products which interest them more than anything else, but they will never know it unless the headline tells them."
> "I often spend hours on a single headline. Often scores of headlines are discarded before the right one is selected."
> "It's not uncommon for a change in headlines to multiply returns from five to ten times over."
> "Your main appeal should lie in your headline." Address the people you seek, and them only. Headlines must never mislead.

The episode's model is Jobs' "1,000 songs in your pocket": the whole value proposition in a handful of words, and you want to hear the rest.

**Apply:**
- Spend most of the effort on headlines. For each angle, write 15–25 candidates, then keep the best 2–3 and say why.
- Call out the reader by name or role ("Realtors:", "Mortgage agents in Ontario:", "Courtiers au Québec :").
- Put the single best promise in the headline, not in the body or the description.
- Seed: "For [one reader], SocialSpark [one concrete result]." Prefer the "1,000 songs" shape: concrete, a little surprising, provable (e.g. "Your listing link is already a carousel").
- Test headlines before anything else; they move results more than any other element.

## 11. Be specific: actual figures are believed, platitudes are not

> "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck."
> "Best in the world, lowest prices… are all at best simply claiming the expected… Actual figures are not generally discounted."

A clothier who said "My net profit is 3%" beat everyone saying "lowest prices". A razor maker who advertised "a 78-second shave" beat everyone saying "quick shaves", because a precise figure implies an actual test.

**Apply:** replace every adjective with a measured fact. "Fast" → "under a minute"; "lots of options" → "6 post types, 3 platforms, 2 languages"; "accurate" → "semi-annual compounding and CMHC premiums, the 30-year surcharge included". If a sharper number would help but hasn't been measured (median seconds from link to post, posts created, hours saved), recommend measuring it and leave a `[MEASURE: …]` placeholder. Never estimate one.

## 12. Do the research: the keystone fact is buried

> "Genius is the art of taking pains."
> A writer "will often read for weeks on some problem… Perhaps in many volumes he will find few facts to use, but some one fact may be the keystone of his success."
> "The ad seems so simple and it must be simple to appeal. But back of that ad may lie reams of data."

Ogilvy found his most famous Rolls-Royce line ("At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock") in an obscure engineering report.

**Apply:** before drafting, dig through the product itself for the one fact that startles: generation prompts, mortgage math, compliance checks, photo selection, the FAQ, and any customer words available. Write the 3 most surprising facts you found at the top of your working notes. A simple ad sits on top of a lot of digging.

## 13. Give it a personality: people love people, not corporations

> "Personalities appeal, while soulless corporations do not. Make a man famous and you make his creation famous."

Hopkins built brands around real people (e.g. "Professor Anderson") whenever he could.

**Apply:** two ways.
- *The maker:* ads spoken by a real founder ("I built SocialSpark after watching agents…") often beat brand-voice ads. Use a real name and story only if the user provides them; otherwise use `[FOUNDER NAME / STORY]`.
- *The buyer:* SocialSpark's promise is that the post looks and sounds like the **agent**: their name, brokerage, headshot, colours, voice. Sell the agent's personal brand, not our software.

## 14. Sell rewards, not prevention

> "People will do anything to cure a trouble, but little to prevent it… They want to be told of rewards."
> "I have never pictured the afflicted. Every illustration I've ever used… showed attractive people and beautiful teeth." (Pepsodent: sold as a creator of beauty, not a decay preventive. It made Hopkins a million dollars.)

**Apply:**
- Lead with the gain: a polished feed, every listing posted, a broker getting realtor referrals, looking like the most professional agent in the office.
- A *present* trouble is fair to cure ("Still writing captions at 10 p.m.?"). A *future* disaster to prevent ("Don't risk a compliance fine") is weak. Frame broker compliance as a reward: "Every sheet ready to share: licence number, rate date and disclaimer already on it."
- Creative shows beautiful finished posts and sheets, not stressed agents or empty feeds.

## 15. Forget yourself entirely

> "I have seen many an ad killed by a single unfortunate phrase, usually a selfish phrase." Examples: "insist on this brand", "avoid imitations", "look out for substitution".
> "Say only what you think a good salesman should say if that prospect stood before him. Do not boast."

**Apply:** cut any phrase that serves us and not the reader: "accept no substitutes", "don't settle for Canva", "join the revolution", "the only…", jabs at competitors. One selfish phrase can sink an otherwise good ad.

## 16. The salesman test, and "the more you tell, the more you sell"

> "Advertising is multiplied salesmanship… A mediocre salesman may affect a small part of your business. Mediocre advertising affects it all."
> "Ask yourself, would this help a salesman sell the goods? Would it help me sell if I met the buyer in person?"
> "Some tell you to be very brief… Would you confine [a salesman] to a certain number of words?… Give them enough to get action."

The headline earns attention. After that the reader is a half-sold prospect asking questions, and length helps if every line answers one.

**Apply:**
- Check every line by asking whether a good salesman would say it to an agent across the desk. If not, cut it.
- Meta: always include a long primary-text variant (problem → demonstration → pains taken → risk reversal → CTA) and test it against the short one. Never assume short wins.
- Google: use all 15 headlines, all 4 descriptions and every asset type. That is the "tell more" inside fixed limits.

## 17. The best ads ask no one to buy: coax, don't drive

> "The best ads ask no one to buy. The ads are based entirely on service. They offer wanted information."
> A brush company's 2,000 door-to-door salesmen said "I was sent here to give you a brush… take your choice." Choosing one, she saw others she wanted, wanted to reciprocate, and ordered.
> "People can be coaxed but not driven. Whatever they do, they do to please themselves."

**Apply:** CTAs offer something rather than ask for money: "See your listing as a post", "Build your first feature sheet free", not "Subscribe today". Let the free posts be the gift that starts reciprocity. The paid plan comes after the demonstration.

## 18. Use fixed human psychology

> "Human nature is perpetual… The principles of psychology are fixed and enduring."

Levers named in the episode:
- **Curiosity** is "one of the strongest human incentives". An open loop in a headline ("What one listing link turns into") works if the body pays it off honestly.
- **People judge by price.** Value is judged by what something costs, so give comparisons that frame the price honestly (e.g. a month of posts vs. one hour of a designer). Only make comparisons you can support.
- **Offers limited to a group** outperform general offers: people "will go a long way not to lose" an advantage their group is entitled to. "Built for Canadian realtors", "For mortgage agents and brokers", "Pour les courtiers du Québec". Never invent a fake exclusive deal.
- **The same offer framed differently** can multiply returns, so test framings of one offer, not only different offers.

## 19. Once an ad works, stick with it

> "As a matter of fact, it's seldom wise to change. The best way to win new customers is usually the way that won millions, but the advertising becomes monotonous to the men who read every ad."

Ogilvy: "You're not advertising to a standing army; you're advertising to a moving parade."

**Apply:** a proven winner is the control. New variants must beat it to replace it. Never change it because *we* are bored. On Meta, if frequency rises and CTR falls, refresh the *creative execution* (new photo or listing, new opening line) while keeping the winning *appeal*.

## 20. Stay close to the money

Hopkins left bookkeeping (the expense side) for selling, because results can be measured and paid for. He bought Pepsodent shares on the strength of his own copy.

**Apply:** every ad has one measurable action and is judged on it. Use separate UTM / ad-group labels per angle so results trace back to the appeal.
